Selected Callsun project references and practical considerations for PV module supply across multiple industrial buildings

**A multi-roof industrial park project is not simply one large rooftop system divided into smaller pieces.**

Each building may have a different roof structure, usable area, orientation, rooftop equipment and electrical arrangement. Module procurement therefore has to support several installation conditions within one overall project scope.

This portfolio brings together selected multi-roof industrial project references from Callsun. Project names, locations, capacities and individual system details are not disclosed. The images are grouped by project condition to show what buyers, installers and system providers should consider when planning PV module supply across an industrial park.

## One Project Boundary, Several Roof Conditions

Industrial parks commonly include production buildings, warehouses, offices and auxiliary facilities. Even when the buildings are part of the same site, their roofs may not offer the same installation conditions.

Long factory roofs can support continuous rows, while smaller buildings may require shorter arrays or separate installation blocks. Raised roof sections, ridgelines, access routes and reserved areas can further change the usable footprint.

For procurement, the useful starting point is not only the total target capacity. The project team also needs a roof-by-roof breakdown of usable dimensions, planned module count, electrical parameters and delivery sequence.

## Repeated Buildings Still Need Individual Layout Checks

Several buildings may look similar from ground level but differ in roof length, structural divisions, equipment placement or clear access areas. Repeating one layout without checking each roof can create avoidable changes later in the project.

![Multiple factory roofs with distinct module layouts and reserved areas](/blog/2026-09-21-industrial-park-multi-roof-solar-projects-distinct.webp)

The image above shows how module rows may differ between adjacent buildings. Some areas support long, regular arrays; others require gaps around raised roof structures or a more segmented arrangement.

Small differences become significant when repeated across many buildings. Module dimensions affect how many panels fit within each zone, while voltage and current characteristics affect how those groups can be integrated into the planned system design.

## Segmented Roofs Require Controlled Product Consistency

On roofs divided by ridges, raised sections or service corridors, modules may be installed in several separate blocks. The project may therefore involve many repeated installation points rather than one continuous field.

In this type of project, consistency extends beyond rated wattage. Dimensions, frame construction, electrical characteristics, connectors, cables, labeling and packaging all need to follow the agreed product specification.

A change that appears minor at module level can affect mounting, string allocation, handling or replacement planning when it is multiplied across several roofs. Buyers should therefore confirm the exact model and controlled specification before volume supply begins.

## Large Industrial Parks Need a Roof-by-Roof Supply Plan

Where arrays cover several large production buildings, procurement and delivery should follow the actual installation plan. Shipping every module as one undifferentiated quantity may make site handling and allocation more difficult.

Before the order enters the final supply stage, the buyer and supplier should confirm the selected product, order quantity, packaging, shipping method, destination, required documents and delivery conditions. Where appropriate, packaging identification and shipment planning should also reflect how modules will be distributed across the site.

The final site allocation, lifting plan, storage arrangement and installation sequence remain the responsibility of the appropriately qualified project parties. The module supplier should provide accurate product and packing information so those parties can plan from a consistent reference.

## Selecting Modules for Multi-Roof Industrial Projects

Higher output per module can be useful when it reduces module count or helps a project reach its target capacity. It is not automatically the best choice for every roof within an industrial park.

Module length, width, weight and electrical characteristics must be checked against the usable installation zones, handling conditions, mounting plan and inverter design. A product that fits a large warehouse may not produce the same layout efficiency on a smaller office or auxiliary building.

Callsun's current standard PV module portfolio covers 450W, 580W, 620W and 720W power classes for applications including distributed PV and small commercial and industrial projects. These options give project buyers several starting points for evaluation, but the final selection should follow the actual roof and system requirements.

## Manufacturing Control and Model-Specific Documentation

Once a module has been approved, the agreed requirements need to be maintained from production through final release. Callsun uses fully automated production for standard PV modules, covering non-destructive cell cutting, multi-busbar string welding, in-process EL inspection, vision-guided layup, controlled high-temperature vacuum lamination, automated framing and final EL and I-V testing.

EL inspection and I-V testing serve different purposes within the manufacturing-control process. They do not replace project design, site inspection, commissioning or long-term operating assessment, but they provide controlled checks before modules are released for delivery.

Documentation should also be verified for the exact module being purchased. Buyers should review the datasheet, dimensions, electrical parameters, mechanical construction, connector information and applicable documents at model level rather than assuming that one document covers every product in the same power range.
